Azenta Life Sciences · CryoArc™ Pico

Sliding Freezer Lid for a -190°C Storage System

The lid that opens and closes the freezer every time the robot reaches for a sample. I designed the full lid subsystem, from concept to cold testing.

My role
Subsystem owner, lid and Y-axis
Environment
LN2 vapor, -190°C below the lid
Product
CryoArc™ Pico, released

The challenge

The CryoArc Pico stores biological samples above liquid nitrogen. The lid must open every time the robot picks or places a sample, and close tightly in between to keep the cold in. Three things made it hard:

  • Very little space. The Pico is a compact system, so the lid and its drive had to fit in a tight envelope.
  • Cold LN2 vapor. Vapor rising from the freezer chills everything above it, and very cold parts become brittle or bind.
  • Condensation. Moisture from room air condenses and freezes on cold surfaces, which can block the lid or ruin the seal.

My approach

I used what I learned from testing other lid designs at Azenta. Because space was so tight, I chose a sliding lid over other ways of opening it. Then I solved the cold and condensation problems:

  1. A material that handles the cold. I made the lid from a hard plastic instead of rubber, because it performs better at low temperatures.
  2. Keep the lid warmer. I directed the cold LN2 vapor away from the lid, so the lid stays warmer and less frost forms.
  3. Self-cleaning. The sliding action wipes condensation off on every cycle, so moisture can't build up into ice.

What I designed

I designed the whole lid subsystem: the lid, its guides, and the drive. I sized the motor, lead screw, and bearings for the load, speed, and cycle life the product needed.

Testing and validation

  1. Bench
    Cycle test at room temperature. Ran the lid for the required number of cycles on the bench. Passed.
  2. Cold
    Cycle test on a real freezer. Installed the lid on a freezer with cold LN2 vapor so it ran at real operating temperature, then cycle tested again. Passed.
  3. Seal
    Temperature mapping. Mapped temperatures around the lid to confirm it seals the freezer well enough to hold the cold.

Result

The lid passed design verification in both room-temperature and cold testing, and is part of the released CryoArc™ Pico.

I also designed the Pico's Y-axis, which is not covered on this page.

Azenta Life Sciences · CryoArc™ Deca and Tera

Fixing Cracked Freezer Lids in the Field

Freezer lids were cracking on customer units of two large -190°C storage systems. I found the root cause and designed a fix that could be added to freezers already in the field.

My role
Sustaining mechanical engineer
Products
CryoArc™ Deca and Tera
Fix released
2023, field retrofit

The challenge

The freezer lid was cracking on units already installed at customer sites. A cracked lid lets warm, moist air into a -190°C freezer, so the problem had to be solved quickly and for good. The lids kept failing, so every unit in the field needed the upgrade. That meant the fix had to be retrofittable: easy to install on freezers already running at customer sites.

Finding the root cause

Instead of guessing, I listed every possible cause and ran an experiment for each one:

  • Temperature test. Measured the lid temperature under worst-case conditions.
  • Condensation test. Simulated worst-case condensation build-up on the lid.

The tests showed that condensation forming and freezing on the lid was the problem.

What I designed

  1. A small surface heater on the lid, so moisture does not freeze on it.
  2. A small chamber on top of the lid that collects condensation and directs it away, so it cannot build up and freeze on the lid.

Both parts were designed to be added to existing freezers as a field retrofit.

Testing and validation

  1. Thermal
    Heater does not warm the freezer. Thermal testing confirmed the new heater does not affect the storage temperature inside the freezer.
  2. Cycle
    Added weight is safe for the drive. Cycle testing confirmed the extra weight on the lid does not affect the motor and drive mechanism that open and close it.

Result

Zero lid failures reported from the field since the new design was released in 2023.

Moving XPeel® Production to Taiwan

A benchtop instrument that removes seals from lab microplates. I led the mechanical side of moving its manufacturing from the U.S. to Taiwan, and kept it in production when a key motor reached end of life.

My role
Mechanical engineer
Work
Manufacturing transfer, sustaining
Site
U.S. → Taiwan

The challenge

Azenta wanted to build this product in Taiwan to lower its cost. The machine had to stay the same for customers: same function, same reliability. That meant every drawing, part, and assembly step had to work for a new manufacturer and a new supply chain.

Manufacturing transfer

  • Drawings. Reviewed and updated drawings for custom parts that had small differences in Taiwan, such as an internal part in a different color, or an insert from a different manufacturer that works the same way.
  • Commercial parts. Many off-the-shelf parts were bought in the U.S. and had to come from Taiwan suppliers instead. For each one, I made sure it was either the same part from a new supplier, or an alternative part that meets the same functional specification.
  • Assembly instructions. Updated outdated assembly SOPs so the Taiwan team could build the machine correctly.

Replacing an end-of-life motor

The original motor came from the automotive industry, so its price was very low. When it reached end of life, my goal was to change only the motor, with no changes to any other part, and keep the cost about the same.

  • Matched the new motor's torque, speed, and mounting interface to the original.
  • Matched the price, so the product cost stayed about the same.

Testing and validation

  1. Motor
    Motor reliability test. Verified the new motor lasts the full lifetime of the machine.
  2. System
    System reliability test. Tested machines built in Taiwan to verify they were assembled to a quality similar to the U.S.-built machines.

Result

Significantly lower production cost, with Taiwan-built machines passing reliability testing, and the product kept in production after the motor change with no other part changes.

AutoGuide Mobile Robots (Teradyne)

Max-N10 Autonomous Forklift

A heavy-duty autonomous forklift shown at MODEX 2020. I designed how it charges itself, integrated its navigation sensors, and made its steering column cheaper to build.

My role
Mechanical engineer
Shown at
MODEX 2020
Steering cost
-40%

The challenge

An autonomous forklift has to work for hours without a person. It must charge itself, see its surroundings to drive safely, and still be affordable to build. I worked on all three, often in very tight space under and inside the robot.

Self-charging: retractable charging collector

I designed a retractable charging collector so the forklift can charge itself with no one plugging it in. The hard part was where it had to go:

  • Only 2.5 in of ground clearance. The charging contact is on the bottom of the forklift, so the whole mechanism had to fit in that thin space under the robot.
  • Firm contact every time. The mechanism pivots down, then compresses straight down onto the charger to make a firm electrical contact.
  • Retrofittable. It had to fit on existing robots, not only new ones.

I took it from concept through design, prototype, testing, and release.

Became the standard charging collector on all AutoGuide robots.

Seeing the world: sensor integration

Integrated 2D LiDAR and 3D vision hardware into the robot chassis, so the forklift can navigate and detect obstacles safely.

Lower cost: steering column redesign

The original steering column was over-designed, built as a complex weldment. Using design for manufacturing and assembly (DFMA), I replaced it with a much simpler design made from box tube.

Cut steering column production cost by 40%.
